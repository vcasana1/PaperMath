Introduction

The imaginary parts γk of the nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function are located only on
average by the Riemann–von Mangoldt formula: the points τk defined by N0 (τk ) = k − 12 (1.2),
where N0 is the smooth main term of the zero-counting function, discard the oscillating term
S(t) = π1 arg ζ( 21 + it). The primes enter through S. The Euler product writes ζ(s) as a product
of one factor (1−p−s )−1 for each prime p. The Euler phase of the prime p is − π1 arg(1−p−1/2−it ),
the normalized phase of this factor at s = 12 + it, and S(t) is, formally, the sum of the Euler
phases of all primes. Truncated at the primes p ≤ x, with x a small power of t, this sum
approximates S in mean
√ square, unconditionally (Selberg, Tsang, Fujii; see below). Lemma 7.6
extends this to about t primes. The arithmetic correction studied here adds the Euler phases
of finitely many primes to the main term (Φm in (1.10), interpolated as Φ in (1.11)). The zeros
are then approximated by points τkC attached to the levels N0 + Φ = k − 12 , instead of the roots
τk of N0 (t) = k − 21 .
The main result constructs the strictly increasing sequence τkC through the sublevel measures
(1.12) of N0 +Φ, where Φ is defined by the Euler phases of finitely many primes. Unconditionally,
the root-mean-square error of τkC with respect to γk on dyadic blocks is O(1/ log T ), a bounded
multiple of the mean zero spacing (Theorem 1.5). The key technical feature is the sublevel-set
definition: it remains valid even when the prime-corrected counting function N0 + Φ is not
monotone, where the equation N0 + Φ = k − 21 may have no unique root.
An exact defect identity supplies an independent
√ comparison. Without RH, the uncorrected
points τk differ from the γk by root-mean-square 2 log log T / log T (1+o(1)), the factor log log T
coming from Selberg’s mean square of S. Thus the arithmetic correction removes the factor

the arithmetic correction itself and the defect of the ordinal equation when evaluated at the
actual imaginary parts γk .

second concerns its approximation by the Euler phases of the primes.

Two prime-based constructions provide a comparison with Theorem 1.5. LeClair [24, §VI,
eq. (49)] replaces S by the phase of a finite number of Euler factors, that number being a free
numerical parameter, and computes very high zeros by root-finding near the previous approximation. The aim there is numerical, and no error bound is stated. Gonek [13] builds functions
ζX from a smoothed Euler product over n ≤ X 2 (in this paragraph X is his parameter); for
large t the zeros of ζX ( 12 + it) are the points where a phase function built from primes crosses
the levels (2k + 1)π [13, §10]. His results on these zeros assume RH: their number and simplicity
when X is small with respect to t [13, Thm. 8.1], and their convergence to the imaginary parts
of the zeros of ζ as X → ∞, on a fixed compact interval and without a rate [13, Thm. 9.1].

1. it is unconditional;
2. it is quantitative: the root-mean-square error is O(1/ log T ), and Corollary 1.6 bounds the
proportion of large deviations;

3. the number of primes is tied to the height, about t primes at height t, instead of being

By comparison, Theorem 1.5 controls only the mean square, whereas Gonek’s Theorem 9.1,
under RH, concerns every imaginary part.

At a level where F is not monotone, the equation may have several solutions. If solutions are
indexed by counting, every spurious pair shifts all the later indices, so the errors accumulate
along the sequence. Theorem 1.5 neither proves nor uses monotonicity of F. The number τkC is
the distribution function λ 7→ meas{t > 0 : F(t) < λ} evaluated at λ = k − 21 . Equivalently, it is
the position at which the increasing rearrangement of F reaches this level, with the generalizedinverse convention ([15, §10.12] define the rearrangement in decreasing order as the inverse of
the distribution function; the increasing one is defined in the same way). If F is replaced on
a bounded interval I by another function with the same minimum and maximum on I, then
τkC changes only for the levels k − 12 with minI F < k − 12 ≤ maxI F: for the other levels
meas(I ∩ {F < k − 21 }) is 0 or |I| in both cases. Theorem 7.3, which holds for every measurable
f , bounds the total effect by the fourth moment of ⌊F + 12 ⌋ − N and the third moment of the
gaps. The sublevel construction thus takes the place of a monotonicity argument.
Precedents of the construction. The definition (1.12) evaluates the distribution function
DF (λ) = meas{t > 0 : F(t) < λ} at λ = k− 12 . With the standard generalized-inverse convention,
the increasing rearrangement reaches this level at the position DF (k − 12 ) (Remark 7.4).
The imaginary parts themselves are the same construction applied to N , since γk =
meas{N < k − 21 }, and Theorem 7.3 compares the two. Rearrangements and quantile functions
are classical [15, Ch. X]. An application of this construction to the imaginary parts of the zeros,
or an inequality of the form (7.5), is not known to the author to have been published as of 30
October 2026.
As of 30 October 2026, the author is not aware of published results that combine the unconditional construction by the Euler phases of finitely many primes, increasing sublevel-defined
approximations of the imaginary parts, and the mean-square localization in Theorem 1.5. The
bounds in Theorems 1.1 and 1.3 are compared quantitatively with their cited antecedents above.

Conclusion

The principal conclusion is the unconditional, fully ordinally indexed construction τkC from
finitely many Euler phases. The sublevel measures remain strictly increasing even though the
underlying prime-corrected counting function need not be monotone. Theorem 1.5 proves a rootmean-square localization error O(1/ log T ) over the complete list of zeros, including multiplicities.
The exact defect identity provides a separate unconditional asymptotic: the uncorrected
Riemann–von Mangoldt points τk have mean-square error 2 log log T / log2 T (1 + o(1)). The
arithmetic correction removes the divergent factor log log T from the proved upper bound of the
mean square, which is O(1/ log2 T ) for the corrected points; no asymptotic of the corrected error
is claimed. Proposition 9.1 shows that the correction has the same leading second moment and
is asymptotically aligned with the actual displacement vector.
Theorem 9.3 and Corollary 9.4 demonstrate the related bounded ordinal defect and the
leading second-moment asymptotic of the prime phases at the imaginary parts.
Independently, the argument estimates yield lim supk |τk − γk | ≤ 0.60832 and the explicit
bound |S(T )| ≤ 0.09783 log T +0.7214 log log T +10.6 for T ≥ e. The improved leading coefficient
comes essentially from pointwise reflection by the functional equation on the left of the critical
line. As explained in Section 1.2, it improves the complete estimate of Bellotti–Wong only
beyond the stated crossing height.
Theorem 9.5 gives a deterministic criterion for certifying simple critical-line zeros from individual ordinal defects. The available bounds do not satisfy the numerical threshold required to
deduce RH, simplicity, or any stated positive proportion from this certificate.
Limitations of this work. The constant in Theorem 1.5 is not explicit, so the result gives
no numerical guarantee at any finite height; it controls the mean square, not every individual
imaginary part, and the explicit bound of Theorem 1.3 improves on Bellotti–Wong only for
log T > 2425.8385 . . ., as it trades the constant term for the leading coefficient (for comparison,
(1.8) is smaller than the bound |N (t)−N0 (t)| ≤ 0.11 log t+0.29 log log t+2.29+0.2/t0 , t ≥ t0 ≥ e,
used by Yang [44, (4.29)] in the proof of his zero-free region, as soon as log t > 933.5).
An explicit version of Theorem 1.5 would require explicit forms of Selberg’s approximation
and of Fujii’s theorem.
