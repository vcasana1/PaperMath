1    Introduction
The imaginary parts γk of the nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function are located only on
average by the Riemann–von Mangoldt formula: the points τk defined by N0 (τk ) = k − 12 (1.2),
where N0 is the smooth main term of the zero-counting function, discard the oscillating term
S(t) = π1 arg ζ( 21 + it). The primes enter through S. The Euler product writes ζ(s) as a product
of one factor (1−p−s )−1 for each prime p. The Euler phase of the prime p is − π1 arg(1−p−1/2−it ),
the normalized phase of this factor at s = 12 + it, and S(t) is, formally, the sum of the Euler
phases of all primes. Truncated at the primes p ≤ x, with x a small power of t, this sum
approximates S in mean square, unconditionally
                                      √             (Selberg [39], Tsang [44], Fujii [13]; see below).
Lemma 7.6 extends this to about t primes. The arithmetic correction studied here adds the
Euler phases of finitely many primes to the main term (Φm in (1.10), interpolated as Φ in (1.11)).
The zeros are then approximated by points τkC attached to the levels N0 + Φ = k − 12 , instead of
the roots τk of N0 (t) = k − 21 .



                                                  2
    The main result constructs the strictly increasing sequence τkC through the sublevel measures
(1.12) of N0 +Φ, where Φ is defined by the Euler phases of finitely many primes. Unconditionally,
the root-mean-square error of τkC with respect to γk on dyadic blocks is O(1/ log T ), at most
a bounded multiple of the mean zero spacing (Theorem 1.5). The key technical feature is the
sublevel-set definition: it remains valid even when the prime-corrected counting function N0 + Φ
is not monotone, where the equation N0 + Φ = k − 12 may have no unique root.
    An exact defect identity supplies an independent   √ comparison. Without RH, the uncorrected
points τk differ from the γk by root-mean-square 2 log log T / log T (1+o(1)), the factor log log T
coming from Selberg’s mean square of S [39]. Thus the arithmetic correction removes the factor
log log T from the proved mean-square upper order. Further consequences proved below describe
the arithmetic correction itself and the defect of the ordinal equation when evaluated at the
actual imaginary parts γk .
    The other results concern the argument S(T ) of the zeta function. A profile-based applica-
tion of Backlund’s method yields an asymptotic coefficient 0.0968160 . . ., a resulting upper limit
0.60832 for the distance between the points τk of the Riemann–von Mangoldt equation and the
actual imaginary parts γk , and a fully explicit estimate |S(T )| ≤ 0.09783 log T +0.7214 log log T +
10.6. The leading coefficient is smaller than that of Bellotti and Wong [4], obtained by Back-
lund’s method; the complete estimate improves upon theirs only for log T > 2425.8385 . . .. The
estimates of Turing type of Amberger [1] and of Bellotti and Wong [5] are smaller (Section 1.3).

    Several related approaches supplement the main term N0 (T ) = 2π       log 2πe + 87 of the Riemann–

the correcting term and in whether RH is assumed.
    For approaches that use ζ, explicit bounds in the critical strip (Yang [47]; Bellotti [3]) give
unconditional bounds for S(T ) and hence for N − N0 (Trudgian [42], [43]; Bellotti and Wong [4,
Thm. 1.1, Cor. 1.5]). These control the size of N − N0 , not the position of the imaginary parts.
    França and LeClair [10, eq. (20)] write the n-th imaginary part as a solution of N0 (t)+S(t) =
n − 21 with the exact S; they do not assume RH, but they assume that limδ→0+ arg ζ( 12 + δ + it)
exists for every t, and they do not prove that the equation has exactly one solution for each n
[10, §9]. In approaches using primes, S is approximated by a sum over primes p ≤ x, in mean
square and unconditionally (Selberg [39], Tsang [44], Fujii [13]).
    Kargin [24, Thm. 1.1] takes tk with N0 (tk ) = k − 21 and proves, without RH, that γk − tk ,
suitably normalized, is asymptotically Gaussian (for his enumeration of the zeros, which agrees
with the one used here when RH holds; see Section 1.2), a limit law over windows of k and not
a bound for each imaginary part.
    Korolev [25, Thm. 4.2] proves, without RH, Selberg’s formulas for the moments of the Gram
displacements ∆G    k , the integers that locate each γk among the Gram points, where the smooth
term N0 takes integer values up to the Stirling remainder.
    Gonek [15] builds from a finite Euler product a function ζX whose zeros up to height t

At t ∈ {γk } this paper uses the right-hand convention S(t) = S(t+ ), compatible with the
right-continuous counting function N . Different conventions are used in the literature (see [41,

                                         N0 (τk ) = k − 21 .                                 (1.2)
The sequence (τk ) solves the counting equation with the oscillating term S removed. In terms
of the Gram points gm , defined by ϑ(gm ) = mπ for m ≥ −1 (g−1 = 9.66 . . .), one has N0 (gm ) =
m + 1 − ϖ(gm )/π, because ϑ/π + 1 = N0 + ϖ/π (Lemma 4.1). Since |ϖ|/π < 12 , we have
N0 (gk−2 ) < k − 12 < N0 (gk−1 ) for every k ≥ 1. Strict monotonicity thus gives gk−2 < τk < gk−1 .
The imaginary part γk lies in the same Gram interval, γk ∈ (gk−2 , gk−1 ], exactly when the
displacement ∆G  k of Remark 7.7 vanishes. This is Gram’s law in the index convention adopted
here for the k-th imaginary part (the Gram–Selberg phenomenon of [25, Def. 1.4]), and it fails
for a positive proportion of k [25, p. 58]. The resulting sequence gives a continuous counterpart
of the Gram scale, read at the half-integer levels.
    The object of this paper is to determine how far these points lie from the imaginary parts γk ,
and how much arithmetic information can bring them, in root-mean-square, within a bounded
number of mean spacings 2π/ log γk , with the intermediate objective of obtaining a better ap-
proximation of τk (or modifications thereof) to γk . The first question concerns the size of S; the
second concerns its approximation by the Euler phases of the primes.

At a level where F is not monotone, the equation may have several solutions. If solutions are
indexed by counting, every spurious pair shifts all the later indices, so the errors accumulate
along the sequence. Theorem 1.5 neither proves nor uses monotonicity of F. The number τkC is
the distribution function λ 7→ meas{t > 0 : F(t) < λ} evaluated at λ = k − 21 . Equivalently, it is
the position at which the increasing rearrangement of F reaches this level, with the generalized-
inverse convention ([17, §10.12] define the rearrangement in decreasing order as the inverse of
the distribution function; the increasing one is defined in the same way). If F is replaced on
a bounded interval I by another function with the same minimum and maximum on I, then
τkC changes only for the levels k − 12 with minI F < k − 12 ≤ maxI F: for the other levels
meas(I ∩ {F < k − 21 }) is 0 or |I| in both cases. Theorem 7.3, which holds for every measurable
f , bounds the total effect by the fourth moment of ⌊F + 12 ⌋ − N and the third moment of the
gaps. The sublevel construction thus takes the place of a monotonicity argument.

Remark 7.4 (Quantile form). In this remark mf and mN are local. Let mf be the image of
Lebesgue measure on [a, b] under f , a measure of mass b − a on R, and mN the image under N ,

mf (−∞, k − 21 ) and γk − a = mN (−∞, k − 21 ) : the left side of (7.5) compares the distribution

counting levels (with multiplicities, N may skip levels). Equivalently, xfk is the point where the
increasing rearrangement of f on [a, b] ([17, §10.12], in increasing order), the generalized inverse
of the distribution function, reaches the level k − 21 , and γk is the same point for N . The left
side of (7.5) is thus a discrete form of the squared L2 distance between the two distribution
functions. It is not the squared Wasserstein distance W22 , which compares their inverses, the
increasing rearrangements of f and N . The right side has no counterpart known to the author:
one factor is a moment of the gaps Dℓ , which are, up to the truncation at a and b, the masses
of the atoms of mN (the atoms themselves sit at the integers ℓ), the other a moment of the
pointwise difference f − N .

10     Conclusion
The principal conclusion is the unconditional, fully ordinally indexed construction τkC from
finitely many Euler phases. The sublevel measures remain strictly increasing even though the
underlying prime-corrected counting function need not be monotone. Theorem 1.5 proves a root-
mean-square localization error O(1/ log T ) over the complete list of zeros, including multiplicities.
    The exact defect identity provides a separate unconditional asymptotic: the uncorrected
Riemann–von Mangoldt points τk have mean-square error 2 log log T / log2 T (1 + o(1)). The
arithmetic correction removes the divergent factor log log T from the proved upper bound of the
mean square, which is O(1/ log2 T ) for the corrected points; no asymptotic of the corrected error


                                                        31
is claimed. Proposition 9.1 shows that the correction has the same leading second moment and
is asymptotically aligned with the actual displacement vector.
    Theorem 9.3 and Corollary 9.4 demonstrate the related bounded ordinal defect and the
leading second-moment asymptotic of the prime phases at the imaginary parts.
    Independently, the argument estimates yield lim supk |τk − γk | ≤ 0.60832 and the explicit
bound |S(T )| ≤ 0.09783 log T +0.7214 log log T +10.6 for T ≥ e. The improved leading coefficient
comes essentially from pointwise reflection by the functional equation on the left of the critical
line. As explained in Section 1.2, it improves the complete estimate of Bellotti–Wong [4] only
beyond the stated crossing height. Both estimates are larger than the bound of Turing type of
Bellotti–Wong [5] (Section 1.3).
    Theorem 9.5 gives a deterministic criterion for certifying simple critical-line zeros from indi-
vidual ordinal defects. The available bounds do not satisfy the numerical threshold required to
deduce RH, simplicity, or any stated positive proportion from this certificate.

Limitations of this work. The constant in Theorem 1.5 is not explicit, so the result gives
no numerical guarantee at any finite height; it controls the mean square, not every individual
imaginary part, and the explicit bound of Theorem 1.3 improves on the bound of Bellotti–Wong
[4] obtained by Backlund’s method only for log T > 2425.8385 . . ., as it trades the constant term
for the leading coefficient, and it does not improve on the bounds of Turing type of [1] and
[5] (for comparison, (1.8) is smaller than the bound |N (t) − N0 (t)| ≤ 0.11 log t + 0.29 log log t +
2.29 + 0.2/t0 , t ≥ t0 ≥ e, used by Yang [47, (4.29)] in the proof of his zero-free region, as soon
as log t > 933.5).
    An explicit version of Theorem 1.5 would require explicit forms of Selberg’s approximation
and of Fujii’s theorem.

def pl(nodes):
    """piecewise linear function through the nodes (sigma, e), increasing sigma, 0 to the right of the last
     node"""
    def e(s):
        if s >= nodes[-1][0]: return mpf(0)
        for (a,ea),(b,eb) in zip(nodes, nodes[1:]):
             if a <= s <= b: return ea + (eb-ea)*(s-a)/(b-a)
        raise ValueError(s)
    return e

def reflect(e):
    """reflected extension"""
    def es(s):
        s = mpf(s)
        if s >= 1: return mpf(0)
        if s >= mpf(1)/2: return e(s)
        if s >= 0: return mpf(1)/2 - s + e(1-s)
        return mpf(1)/2 - s
    return es

def phi_m(m, t):
    """Phi_m(t) = -(1/pi) sum_{nu<=m} arg(1 - p_nu^{-1/2-it})."""
    s = np.zeros_like(t)
    for p in primes[:m]:
        z = p ** (-0.5) * np.exp(-1j * t * np.log(p))
        s -= np.angle(1 - z)
    return s / np.pi
